Drug testing and psychedelics: what employers can and cannot do

For Oregon employers who want to understand what drug testing for psilocybin is legally permissible, what Oregon law restricts, and how to structure a compliant drug-free workplace policy that accounts for the state’s licensed psilocybin program.

The short answer

Oregon employers can maintain drug-free workplace policies, conduct drug testing, and take employment action based on impairment. What they cannot do — under ORS 659A.315 — is use a positive drug test result alone as evidence that an employee was impaired at work, or discipline or terminate an employee solely because they lawfully used psilocybin through Oregon’s licensed program outside of work hours. The practical reality is that standard drug test panels do not detect psilocybin or psilocin, so most employers will not encounter a positive psilocybin result in routine testing. Where an employer is subject to federal drug-free workplace requirements, federal law governs and Oregon’s state-law restrictions do not override it. Understanding which framework applies — state or federal — is the starting point for any employer assessing their drug testing policy in light of Oregon’s psilocybin program.

What standard drug tests detect — and do not detect

The SAMHSA-5 panel, used in federal workplace drug testing and widely adopted by private employers, tests for: marijuana metabolites (THC-COOH), cocaine metabolites, amphetamine and methamphetamine, opiate metabolites, and phencyclidine (PCP). Psilocybin and psilocin are not included.

Extended 10-panel and 12-panel tests add benzodiazepines, barbiturates, methadone, and other substances — but still do not routinely include psilocybin or psilocin. Detecting psilocin requires a specifically targeted immunoassay or mass spectrometry assay that is not part of standard employment screening panels.

Psilocin — psilocybin’s active metabolite — is detectable in urine for approximately 8 to 24 hours after ingestion. At higher doses, detection may extend somewhat beyond that window. Hair follicle testing can theoretically capture a longer exposure history, but hair testing for psilocybin is not standard practice and analytical methods are less established than for other substances. For most employers using standard panels, a positive psilocybin result will simply not occur in routine testing regardless of whether an employee accessed Oregon’s program.

What Oregon law prohibits employers from doing

ORS 659A.315 establishes two protections relevant to employers conducting drug testing in the psilocybin context.

First, an employer may not discipline or terminate an employee solely because the employee lawfully used psilocybin pursuant to ORS 475A outside the workplace and during nonworking hours. The word “solely” is doing significant legal work here. An employer who takes action because an employee used psilocybin and also appeared at work impaired is acting on impairment — not solely on use. An employer who takes action because an employee’s psilocybin use violated a validly applicable policy may have a defensible basis beyond the use itself. But an employer who fires an employee with no other basis than off-duty lawful psilocybin access has violated ORS 659A.315.

Second, an employer may not use a positive drug test result alone as evidence of impairment at work for substances lawfully used under Oregon law. This provision is significant: it means that even if an employer’s test panel happened to detect psilocin, that positive result — standing alone — does not establish that the employee was impaired while working. The employer would need independent evidence of actual workplace impairment to take action on impairment grounds.

What employers can still do

ORS 659A.315’s protections do not eliminate an employer’s right to maintain a drug-free workplace or to take action based on actual impairment.

An employer can prohibit employees from being impaired at work. If an employee reports to work showing signs of impairment — regardless of the substance — the employer can take action based on the observable impairment. Psilocybin’s acute effects last six to eight hours, and residual effects including cognitive changes, fatigue, and altered perception can persist one to two days after a full-dose session. An employer whose employee returns to work the morning after a session while still experiencing these effects has a legitimate impairment basis for action.

An employer can require employees in safety-sensitive roles to meet fitness-for-duty standards and can remove employees from safety-sensitive duties when impairment is reasonably suspected, regardless of the source of impairment.

An employer can maintain a drug-free workplace policy that references federally scheduled substances and can discipline employees for violating it — provided the policy is applied consistently and the discipline is not based solely on lawful off-duty use. The “solely” carve-out in ORS 659A.315 means that a policy-based termination may be defensible where the policy is the actual basis for action rather than the off-duty use itself. This remains unsettled territory; no Oregon appellate court has drawn the precise line between a lawful policy-based termination and a prohibited solely-for-use termination in the psilocybin context as of early 2026.

Federally mandated drug-free workplace programs

Oregon’s state-law employment protections do not override federal drug-free workplace requirements. Employers subject to federal mandates operate under federal law, under which psilocybin is a Schedule I controlled substance regardless of Oregon’s authorization.

The Drug-Free Workplace Act of 1988 applies to federal agencies and to federal contractors and grant recipients above specified thresholds. Employers in these categories must maintain drug-free workplace programs, require employees to notify the employer of any drug conviction, and impose sanctions for policy violations. A psilocybin-positive test result under a federally mandated program creates federal compliance consequences that ORS 659A.315 cannot reach.

DOT-regulated employers — airlines, trucking companies, railroads, pipelines, transit agencies, and others regulated by the FAA, FMCSA, FRA, FTA, or PHMSA — conduct safety-sensitive drug testing under 49 CFR Part 40. DOT testing panels are federally mandated and do not include psilocybin as of early 2026, but the testing framework is governed by federal regulation and the employer’s compliance obligations run to federal agencies. An employee in a DOT safety-sensitive position who tests positive for any federally prohibited substance in a federal test faces consequences under federal DOT rules regardless of ORS 659A.315.

Voluntary employer drug-free workplace policies

An employer who is not subject to federal drug-free workplace requirements but maintains a voluntary drug-free workplace policy should review whether that policy is written to reach lawful off-duty psilocybin use — and if so, whether that is the employer’s actual intent.

A policy written to prohibit “use, possession, or being under the influence of illegal drugs” should be evaluated in light of Oregon’s psilocybin authorization: psilocybin use through a licensed service center is not illegal under Oregon law. A policy written to prohibit use of “federally scheduled substances” or “controlled substances under federal law” reaches psilocybin regardless of state authorization. The drafting matters for both enforcement and litigation.

Employers who want to update their policies to expressly address Oregon’s licensed psilocybin program — either to explicitly include it in prohibited conduct or to explicitly carve it out — should do so in consultation with employment counsel to ensure the policy is both enforceable and consistent with ORS 659A.315.

Reasonable suspicion and impairment testing

Because standard drug tests do not reliably detect psilocybin and psilocin’s detection window is short, most employers who suspect psilocybin-related impairment will be working with behavioral evidence rather than test results. Oregon employers conducting reasonable-suspicion impairment assessments should document observable indicators — changes in behavior, coordination, speech, response time, or other performance markers — independent of any drug test result.

Impairment testing technologies — devices that measure cognitive performance or reaction time rather than substance metabolites — are an emerging category that some employers use alongside or instead of chemical testing. These tools assess current functional capacity rather than prior substance use, and their use does not implicate ORS 659A.315’s restrictions on using positive test results as evidence of impairment.

Employee assistance programs and self-disclosure

An employee who self-discloses psilocybin use to an employer’s EAP or HR department raises a distinct set of questions. ORS 659A.315’s protection applies to the underlying use — the employer cannot discipline solely because the employee disclosed lawful off-duty use. However, an employer who learns through self-disclosure that an employee’s psilocybin use is creating workplace fitness concerns has potentially independent grounds for fitness evaluation, particularly in safety-sensitive roles.

When public information may be enough

ORS 659A.315 is publicly available through the Oregon Legislative Assembly. The Oregon Bureau of Labor and Industries publishes employer guidance on Oregon’s psilocybin employment protection at oregon.gov/boli. SAMHSA publishes current federal workplace drug testing guidelines at samhsa.gov. DOT testing regulations are at 49 CFR Part 40. The Drug-Free Workplace Act provisions for federal contractors and grant recipients are at 41 U.S.C. § 8102.

When you should speak with a lawyer

An employer who wants to review and update their drug-free workplace policy to address Oregon’s licensed psilocybin program should consult employment counsel before making changes. An employer who has taken or is considering taking adverse employment action against an employee based on psilocybin use or a psilocybin-related test result should get legal advice before acting. An employer who is uncertain whether their federal funding or contracting status subjects them to mandatory federal drug-free workplace requirements should consult counsel to assess their specific obligations.
