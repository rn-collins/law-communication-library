Service Centers and Facilitator Licenses: How the Regulated Model Works

Last reviewed: August 2026 · Psychedelic law changes quickly — verify current status before relying on this page.

Who this is for: People trying to understand the structure behind Oregon’s and Colorado’s licensed psilocybin programs — what licenses exist, who holds them, how the supply chain works, and what a service center or healing center actually is legally. Also useful for practitioners, founders, and investors trying to understand what the regulated model permits and where its limits are.

The short answer

Both Oregon and Colorado operate licensed psilocybin programs built around a common structure: a licensed supply chain that runs from cultivation through testing and manufacturing to administration, and a licensed facilitation role that governs who can supervise sessions and under what conditions. The specific license types, regulatory agencies, and operational rules differ between the two states, but the underlying model is the same. Psilocybin can only be administered to participants at a licensed facility, by a licensed facilitator, using product sourced from licensed manufacturers and verified by licensed laboratories. Nothing in that chain can be substituted with unlicensed alternatives. Understanding how the licensed model works — and where each license type begins and ends — is the prerequisite for understanding what is and is not legally permitted in either state.

The license types

Both states recognize four primary license categories covering the supply chain and service delivery.

Facilitator license. Authorizes an individual to provide natural medicine or psilocybin services to clients or participants. This is the person-facing license — the one that governs who may be present during a session, what they may do, and what their professional duties to the client entail. In Oregon, facilitators are licensed by the Oregon Health Authority (OHA). In Colorado, facilitators are licensed by the Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA) through its Office of Natural Medicine.

Service center license (Oregon) / Healing center license (Colorado). Authorizes a physical facility to host administration sessions. This is the location license — it governs where psilocybin may be administered and sets the conditions the facility must meet for safety, security, and regulatory compliance. In Oregon, service centers are licensed by OHA. In Colorado, healing centers are licensed by the Department of Revenue (DOR).

Manufacturer license. Authorizes cultivation, processing, and production of psilocybin products. This is the supply chain license — it governs who may grow, extract, or otherwise produce the substances used in sessions. In Oregon, manufacturers are licensed by OHA. In Colorado, cultivation facilities and product manufacturers are licensed by DOR.

Laboratory license. Authorizes independent testing of psilocybin products for potency, contaminants, and safety before products can be sold or administered. In Oregon, laboratories must be accredited by the Oregon Environmental Laboratory Accreditation Program and licensed by OHA. In Colorado, testing facilities are licensed by DOR.

How the supply chain works

Products administered to clients or participants must pass through the full licensed supply chain before reaching a session. In Oregon, a licensed manufacturer cultivates or processes psilocybin products. Those products are then tested by a licensed laboratory to verify potency and screen for contaminants. Products that pass testing are available for purchase by licensed service centers. Service centers acquire products from licensed manufacturers through licensed channels — no unlicensed sourcing is permitted at any step. Participants purchase psilocybin from the service center during the administration session; they cannot purchase in advance or take product off-site.

Colorado’s supply chain operates on the same logic with DOR overseeing each stage. Cultivation facilities grow the raw material. Manufacturers produce consumable products. Testing facilities verify those products. Healing centers acquire verified products and administer them to participants. The chain is closed — unlicensed product cannot enter it at any point.

This structure is why comparisons to cannabis dispensaries are inaccurate. A cannabis dispensary sells product to a customer who takes it home. A psilocybin service center or healing center is not a retail environment — it is a supervised venue where a licensed facilitator administers a licensed product to a participant who remains on-site for the duration of the experience. The facility and the facilitation are legally inseparable.

What a service center or healing center license authorizes

A service center license in Oregon or a healing center license in Colorado authorizes the facility to:

  • Acquire psilocybin products from licensed manufacturers
  • Store those products in compliance with state security and handling requirements
  • Host preparation, administration, and integration sessions conducted by licensed facilitators
  • Operate with licensed facilitators on staff or contracted to work at the facility
  • Collect and report data to the state as required

A service center or healing center license does not authorize the facility to:

  • Sell or distribute psilocybin for off-site consumption
  • Allow administration by unlicensed individuals
  • Host sessions using product sourced outside the licensed supply chain
  • Operate outside the physical location and conditions approved by the state
  • Make therapeutic claims beyond what the state’s regulatory framework supports

Service center licenses in Oregon carry a $10,000 annual fee. The high licensing fee is a direct consequence of the program’s design: Measure 109 required the program to fund itself without tax dollars, which means licensing fees cover the cost of OHA’s regulatory operations and are reflected in what service centers charge clients.

What a facilitator license authorizes

A facilitator license authorizes an individual to provide psilocybin services — meaning preparation sessions, administration sessions, and integration sessions — to clients or participants within the licensed framework. The facilitator must be present throughout the administration session. They may not administer psilocybin outside a licensed service center or healing center, except in the limited circumstances where the applicable state framework permits otherwise.

In Oregon, a facilitator license requires the holder to be 21 or older, hold a high school diploma or equivalent, pass a criminal background check, complete an OHA-approved training program, and pass a licensing examination. Oregon facilitators are not required to hold any prior professional or healthcare credential. The license costs $2,000 annually; veterans and individuals on public assistance pay $1,000.

In Colorado, there are five pathways to facilitator licensure: original licensure (requiring a 150-hour approved training program, 40 hours of supervised practice, and 40 hours of consultation), endorsement from another licensed jurisdiction, legacy healer status, clinical facilitator pathway (for holders of active licenses as medical doctors, psychologists, licensed counselors, nurse practitioners, or physician assistants), and accelerated clinical facilitator pathway. The tiered structure reflects Colorado’s intent to accommodate both trained non-clinicians and licensed healthcare professionals within a single licensing framework.

A facilitator license does not authorize the holder to:

  • Diagnose health conditions
  • Prescribe medications or treatments
  • Exercise the privileges of any other professional license held while providing psilocybin services, unless operating under a state-specific dual licensure framework
  • Provide services outside a licensed facility, except where specifically permitted by the applicable state framework
  • Administer product sourced outside the licensed supply chain

Oregon’s HB 2387, effective January 1, 2026, created a dual licensure track allowing facilitators who also hold licenses from seven specified professional boards to conduct preparation and integration sessions under both their facilitator license and their professional license simultaneously. This is the only context in which a facilitator may exercise a separate professional credential during psilocybin services under Oregon’s rules.

How the two states’ models differ structurally

Single agency vs. split agency. Oregon centralizes all licensing under OHA. Colorado splits regulatory authority — DORA licenses facilitators, DOR licenses facilities and the supply chain. This means a Colorado facilitator and the healing center where they work answer to different regulatory bodies.

Location requirements. Oregon restricts administration to licensed service centers only, with very limited exceptions. Colorado’s framework contemplates a broader range of settings — standard healing centers, micro-healing centers as add-ons to existing healthcare practices, and in limited circumstances private residences. As of early 2026, at-home administration had been restricted by DORA to palliative care situations, but the framework remains more flexible than Oregon’s in design.

Facility caps. Oregon prohibits municipalities from banning service centers but allows them to regulate time, place, and manner. Colorado prohibits local governments from banning healing centers outright.

Group size. Oregon caps group administration sessions at 25 participants. Colorado permits groups of up to 64 participants.

Personal use alongside licensed access. Colorado’s Proposition 122 decriminalized personal cultivation and use of psilocybin on private residential property. Oregon’s program does not include a comparable personal use provision. This means in Colorado, the licensed program exists alongside a legal personal use option; in Oregon, licensed service centers are the only legal access point.

What sits outside the licensed model

The licensed model is not a ceiling — it is a floor. Activities outside the licensed model are not authorized by it. Specifically:

  • A person who facilitates a psilocybin session without a facilitator license is not operating within the licensed model, regardless of what they call themselves or what training they have completed.
  • A location that hosts psilocybin sessions without a service center or healing center license is not a licensed facility, regardless of how it is described.
  • Product sourced outside the licensed supply chain is not licensed product, regardless of how it was obtained.
  • An individual’s personal use decriminalization in Colorado does not extend to organizing or facilitating sessions for others.

The existence of a licensed model in Oregon and Colorado does not create legal authorization for parallel unlicensed activity. The two operate in separate legal categories with separate legal consequences.

When you should speak with a lawyer

You should speak with a lawyer if:

  • You are considering applying for a facilitator, service center, or healing center license and want to understand the full scope of what that license authorizes and requires
  • You are building a business model around the licensed framework and want to verify that each component of your model falls within the authorized structure
  • You are a licensed professional in another field considering a dual licensure arrangement under Oregon’s HB 2387 framework and want to understand the full scope and limits of that structure
  • You have operated in a way that may have crossed from licensed into unlicensed territory and want to understand your exposure
