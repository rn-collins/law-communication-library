What to prepare before your first meeting with a psychedelic lawyer (overview)

Who this is for: Anyone who has a legal question about psychedelics and is deciding whether and how to consult a lawyer, regardless of whether they are a participant, a facilitator, a business, a researcher, or a church leader.

The short answer

A consultation with a psychedelic lawyer works better when you arrive prepared: with a defined question, a sense of the states involved and any deadlines that are running, and the documents or facts that relate to your situation. Most people come with too little information, not too much, so the consultation spends time on facts that could have been gathered in advance. You do not need a complete file before calling — if a deadline is near, gather what you can and book the appointment rather than waiting until everything is organized.

Define your question before you call

The most useful preparation is deciding what you want to know. “Do I have a legal problem?” is a starting point, but “Can I still bring a claim after what happened to me during a session in Oregon in 2023, and is a particular facilitator licensed?” is a question a lawyer can work with in thirty minutes. Write your question in a sentence or two and see whether it holds together. That exercise often reveals what you actually need and tells you whether you have come to the right kind of lawyer. Know which state or states are involved

Psychedelic law is state-specific, so the lawyer’s first question will often be where the relevant events happened and where you are located. Identify those states before the call. If your situation crosses states — you were harmed in Oregon but you live in California, or you want to start a business in Colorado while living in another state — note both, because the rules may differ and the lawyer needs to know which ones apply.

Note any deadline that may be running

Some deadlines cannot be extended and will end your options if they pass. A personal injury or harm claim runs on a statute of limitations that varies by state and by the type of claim; Statute of limitations for psychedelic harm claims: how long do you have to act? covers Oregon’s rules in detail. A licensing board investigation carries a response deadline. A regulatory filing or a business deadline may be fixed by rule. If any of these are in play, tell the lawyer at the start of the consultation, not the end, and do not delay booking because you are still gathering papers.

Gather what relates to your question

Bring what you have. For a participant who was harmed: any documents you signed before the session, notes or records of what happened, communications with the provider, and a record of any harm you experienced. For a facilitator: your license information, the agreements you work under, and any correspondence from a board or regulator. For a business: your entity documents, licensing status or application, the contracts most relevant to your question, and any regulatory correspondence. For a researcher: your IRB and DEA registration status and a description of your protocol. For a church: your organizational documents and a written description of your practice. You do not need all of this; bring what you have and tell the lawyer what you do not have and why.

Write a short timeline

A chronological list of what happened, with dates, is often the most useful thing you can bring. Write down when things occurred, when you learned of a problem, what you did about it, and who was involved. For a business matter, the timeline of when you entered agreements, when something went wrong, and what has happened since does the same work. A lawyer who can see the sequence quickly can focus the consultation on what matters rather than assembling the facts from your recollection under time pressure.

List the people and organizations involved

Write down every person and entity connected to your situation: the facilitator, the service center, a regulator, another owner, an investor, an opposing party. The lawyer needs this list to check for conflicts of interest and to understand who the relevant parties are. A conflict check at the start of the relationship is much easier than discovering one halfway through the consultation.

Plan to be candid

Tell the lawyer the unfavorable facts along with the ones that support your position. A consultation is protected by attorney-client privilege when you are seeking legal advice, and the lawyer’s advice is only as good as the facts it is based on. Leaving out the part you are most worried about is usually the part the lawyer needs most. The same candor applies to your goals and your budget, because a lawyer who knows both can give you a realistic assessment rather than an optimistic one.

When public information may be enough

For general questions about what is legal, how a program works, or what a document does, this library covers many of those answers without a lawyer. If your question has no deadline, no money at stake, no investigation underway, and no dispute open, you may be able to learn what you need from public sources. When you should speak with a lawyer

Preparation is not a reason to delay when something real is at stake. If a deadline is running, a board is investigating you, a dispute is open, money or a license is at risk, or you were harmed, book the consultation and gather what you can before it rather than waiting for the perfect file.
