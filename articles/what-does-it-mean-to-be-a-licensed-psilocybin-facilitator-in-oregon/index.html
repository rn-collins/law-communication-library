What does it mean to be a licensed psilocybin facilitator in Oregon?

Last reviewed: August 2026 · Psychedelic law changes quickly — verify current status before relying on this page.

For people considering facilitation as a practice, or who want to understand what the license actually authorizes.

The short answer

A licensed psilocybin facilitator is an individual authorized by the state to support clients before, during, and after their consumption of a psilocybin product at a licensed service center. The license authorizes facilitation — not treatment, not therapy, and not medical care. It is issued by Oregon Psilocybin Services (OPS) under ORS 475A.325 and must be renewed annually. Colorado has its own separate facilitator license, also operational as of late 2024. Outside of state-licensed programs, no equivalent legal authorization exists anywhere in the United States.

What the license actually authorizes

Oregon’s facilitator license permits the holder to conduct three types of sessions with clients: a preparation session before the administration, the administration session itself, and an optional integration session afterward. Those three session types define the full scope of what the license covers. A facilitator may work at any licensed service center — the license is not tied to a single facility — and a facilitator may also be, but does not have to be, an owner or employee of a service center operator.

The license does not authorize a facilitator to diagnose, prescribe, provide psychotherapy, or offer clinical treatment. OPS describes the facilitator’s approach as “non-directive.” That framing is intentional: a facilitator is not managing a therapeutic protocol. The facilitator supports the client through the experience while using a standard of care that other reasonable facilitators would use under similar circumstances — the standard articulated in OAR 333-333.

What the license does not cover

Facilitating psilocybin experiences outside the licensed program is not authorized by an OPS facilitator license. That means underground sessions, retreat settings abroad, or any administration of psilocybin outside a licensed Oregon or Colorado service center are legally separate questions — and, federally, psilocybin remains a Schedule I controlled substance regardless of state law.

A facilitator license also does not carry over between states. An Oregon-licensed facilitator has no authorization to facilitate in Colorado, and vice versa. Each state has its own requirements.

Who Oregon issues facilitator licenses to

To hold an Oregon facilitator license under ORS 475A.325, an applicant must be at least 21 years old, hold a high school diploma or equivalent, pass a criminal background check, complete an OPS-approved training program, and pass an OPS-administered licensing examination with a score of at least 75%. The residency requirement — which previously required two years of Oregon residency — expired January 1, 2025. Applicants from any state or country may now apply.

No clinical degree or professional license is required. A facilitator does not have to be a therapist, physician, nurse, or counselor. The training and examination requirements stand on their own.

What the training and licensing process looks like

Training programs must have OPS-approved curriculum and must be licensed by the Higher Education Coordinating Commission (HECC) or receive an exemption determination from HECC. Each program sets its own length, cost, and admission process. The training includes core coursework, practicum hours, and a final examination administered by the training program. After completing training, applicants apply through OPS’s online licensing platform (the TLC system), pay a $150 application fee, and take a separate open-book OPS licensing exam.

If approved, the license is valid for one year from the date of issuance. Annual renewal requires four hours of continuing education and a $2,000 renewal fee. Reduced fees of $1,000 are available for veterans, recipients of Supplemental Security Income, food stamp recipients, and Oregon Health Plan enrollees.

What it means in practice

Most facilitators work at licensed service centers that they did not build. Some facilitators have opened or co-own service centers. Some work across multiple centers. A license issued under ORS 475A.325 is explicitly not limited to any one premises, so a facilitator can contract with or be employed by as many licensed service centers as they choose.

As of early 2025, Oregon has roughly 360 licensed facilitators across approximately 30 operating service centers. Data from Q1 2025 shows the majority of licensed facilitators earn more than $95,000 per year and are over 45 years old.

The license does not guarantee employment or client access. Facilitators must find or create their own practice context — typically by contracting with a licensed service center, since individual facilitators cannot legally administer psilocybin outside one.

What Colorado’s facilitator license covers

Colorado issued its first facilitator licenses in December 2024, and the first regulated session took place June 6, 2025. The Colorado license is issued through the Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA) and authorizes facilitation at licensed healing centers. Colorado’s program includes a clinical facilitator track for licensed mental health and medical professionals, in addition to the general facilitator pathway. Group sessions may include up to 64 participants, compared to Oregon’s cap of 25.

For a detailed walkthrough of Colorado’s licensing requirements specifically, see How do I become a licensed facilitator in Colorado?.

When public information may be enough

If you’re researching whether facilitation is a career path you want to pursue, OPS publishes its licensing requirements, the list of training programs with approved curriculum, and its administrative rules at oregon.gov/psilocybin. That information is free and regularly updated. Reading ORS 475A.325 and OAR 333-333 directly will give you the statutory and regulatory basis for what the license requires and what it authorizes.

When you should speak with a lawyer

If you are a licensed healthcare professional weighing how an OPS facilitator license interacts with your existing professional license, the analysis is more involved than what public materials can answer — particularly given HB 2387, which created dual licensure protections effective January 1, 2026. If you are considering structuring a facilitation practice, opening a service center, or entering a contract with a service center operator, those are situations where legal counsel familiar with ORS 475A and OAR 333-333 is worth the time.
