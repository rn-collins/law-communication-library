Insurance for psychedelic businesses: what is available and what is not

Last reviewed: August 2026 · Psychedelic law changes quickly — verify current status before relying on this page.

For founders and operators of licensed psilocybin service centers in Oregon and natural medicine healing centers in Colorado who want to understand what insurance coverage is available, what the specialty market offers, and where gaps remain.

The short answer

The insurance market for licensed psychedelic businesses has developed substantially since Oregon’s program launched in 2023, but it remains a specialty market — not a standard commercial market. Standard commercial insurance policies written for general businesses frequently exclude coverage for activities involving federally scheduled substances, and a service center or healing center operator who assumes their standard general liability or professional liability policy covers their psilocybin operations is likely wrong. A small number of specialty carriers have developed products specifically for state-licensed psychedelic businesses, and those products are the right starting point. The coverage types a service center needs — general liability, professional liability, product liability, sexual abuse and misconduct, and property — are all available in the specialty market, though limits and pricing differ from the standard commercial market. Getting coverage in place before the first session is a licensing and risk management requirement, not an afterthought.

Why standard commercial policies typically do not apply

Most standard commercial general liability policies contain exclusions for claims arising out of the manufacture, distribution, sale, or use of controlled substances. Psilocybin’s Schedule I status under federal law makes it a controlled substance under those exclusions regardless of Oregon or Colorado state authorization. A service center that suffers a client injury and tenders a claim to a standard commercial general liability carrier may find the claim denied on the basis of the controlled substance exclusion — after the policy was accepted and premiums paid.

The same exclusion problem applies to standard professional liability policies. A licensed therapist’s standard malpractice policy typically excludes claims involving Schedule I substances. A facilitator who is also a licensed clinician and assumes their existing malpractice coverage extends to their facilitation work almost certainly has a coverage gap that will surface at the moment it matters most.

The only reliable way to know whether a specific policy covers psilocybin operations is to ask the question explicitly in writing before the policy is issued, and to receive a written confirmation from the carrier — not an oral representation from a broker.

The specialty insurance market

A specialty insurance market for state-licensed psychedelic businesses has emerged since 2023. Carriers and programs that have offered coverage for Oregon and Colorado licensed operations as of early 2026 include:

TheraCover (through The Baldwin Group) — one of the first movers in psychedelic professional liability insurance, offering coverage specifically for psilocybin facilitators and service centers.

Oath Cannabis Insurance — a program originally built for cannabis businesses that extended its product line to cover licensed psychedelic operations.

Relm Insurance — a specialty carrier focused on emerging and regulated industries including cannabis and psychedelics.

AlphaRoot — an insurance brokerage specializing in cannabis and psychedelic businesses, placing coverage with multiple specialty carriers.

R&A PSYINS — a program specifically designed for the psychedelic services industry, offering facilitator and service center coverage.

This market is developing and carrier participation changes — new programs enter, existing ones adjust their appetite or exit. An operator building an insurance program should work with a broker who actively monitors the specialty psychedelic insurance market rather than one who places coverage in the standard commercial market and is unfamiliar with the specialty carriers.

General liability

General liability insurance covers claims for bodily injury and property damage arising from business operations. For a service center, the relevant scenarios include a client who is physically injured on the premises — a fall in the parking lot, an injury during a session from an unsafe environment — or property damage caused by the service center’s operations.

Whether psychological harm constitutes covered “bodily injury” under a general liability policy depends on the specific policy language and applicable state law. Some policies define bodily injury to include mental anguish or psychological harm; others limit coverage to physical injury. For a business whose primary risk involves psychological experiences, the bodily injury definition is a critical policy term that must be reviewed before coverage is bound.

General liability limits for service centers in the specialty market typically start at $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate. Umbrella or excess liability coverage can extend those limits for operators who want higher protection.

Professional liability

Professional liability insurance — also called errors and omissions insurance — covers claims that the business or its facilitators failed to meet the applicable standard of care in providing psilocybin services. This is the coverage most directly relevant to a client who alleges harm from the facilitation itself — a session that caused psychological distress, a facilitator who failed to screen appropriately, a service center that failed to maintain required protocols.

For a service center, professional liability coverage should address both the entity’s liability and the facilitators working under its roof. Whether employed facilitators are covered under the service center’s professional liability policy depends on the specific policy — some policies extend to employees automatically, others require the facilitators to be listed, and others do not cover independent contractors at all. A service center that uses independent contractor facilitators must confirm how its professional liability policy treats their conduct.

A claims-made policy — the most common structure in professional liability — covers claims reported during the policy period, not claims arising from incidents during the policy period. A service center that switches carriers or closes must consider purchasing extended reporting period (tail) coverage to ensure that claims from past sessions can still be reported.

Sexual abuse and misconduct coverage

Standard professional liability and general liability policies commonly exclude claims arising from sexual abuse or misconduct. For a psilocybin service center — where clients are in altered states of heightened vulnerability, where touch occurs as part of facilitation, and where the power dynamics of the relationship create conditions that courts have recognized as particularly risky — sexual abuse and misconduct (SAM) coverage is not optional.

SAM coverage is a separate endorsement or standalone policy that covers claims alleging sexual abuse or misconduct by the service center’s facilitators or employees. In the psilocybin context, the exposure is heightened by: the elimination of the statute of limitations for adult sexual assault claims arising on or after June 26, 2025 under HB 3582 (ORS 12.118); the vulnerability of clients in altered states; and the intimate nature of some facilitation contexts. A service center without SAM coverage is exposed to potentially uncapped civil liability for post-June 2025 incidents.

SAM coverage is available in the specialty psychedelic insurance market and should be a required component of any service center’s insurance program.

Product liability

A psilocybin service center uses psilocybin products manufactured and tested by separately licensed Oregon or Colorado businesses. If a product defect — contamination, mislabeling, incorrect dosage — causes client harm, product liability coverage responds to claims against the service center arising from that defective product.

Product liability is typically available as an endorsement to a general liability policy or as a separate coverage. A service center should confirm that its general liability or product liability coverage applies to the psilocybin products it purchases from licensed manufacturers, and should also review the manufacturer’s product liability coverage and whether the service center is named as an additional insured on that policy.

Property insurance

A service center’s physical premises and contents — furniture, equipment, computers, the administration room setup — are insurable under commercial property insurance. Standard property insurance policies may contain the same controlled substance exclusion found in general liability policies, or may insure the property without reference to the nature of the business. A property policy that explicitly excludes loss arising from operations involving controlled substances may not respond to a fire or theft at a service center.

Specialty carriers offering psychedelic business insurance typically include property coverage in their program offerings. An operator should confirm that the property coverage includes the business’s specific property at the service center location, including any leasehold improvements.

Workers’ compensation

A service center with employees is required under Oregon and Colorado law to carry workers’ compensation coverage. Workers’ compensation is a state-mandated insurance program — not a voluntary specialty market product — and must be obtained through a licensed workers’ compensation carrier or the state fund. Oregon’s workers’ compensation system is administered through the Workers’ Benefit Fund. Colorado’s system operates similarly.

Workers’ compensation insurers may ask about the nature of the business and the work employees perform. A service center should disclose the nature of its psilocybin operations when applying for workers’ compensation coverage and should confirm the carrier will provide coverage for the specific employee roles involved — facilitators, client services staff, administrative personnel.

Business interruption

Business interruption coverage replaces lost income if the service center cannot operate due to a covered property loss — a fire, flood, or other covered event. For a service center whose revenue depends entirely on client sessions at a specific physical location, business interruption coverage is a meaningful risk management tool. It is available as an endorsement to commercial property coverage or as a standalone product.

Building a complete insurance program

A complete insurance program for a licensed psilocybin service center typically includes: general liability; professional liability (with confirmation of facilitator coverage); sexual abuse and misconduct; product liability; commercial property; workers’ compensation (if employees); and business interruption. The appropriate limits for each coverage depend on the size of the operation, the number of facilitators, the volume of sessions, and the specific risk profile of the premises.

An insurance broker who specializes in the psychedelic or cannabis industry is the right person to build this program — not a general commercial lines broker who will struggle to find carriers willing to write the coverage. Operators should ask brokers directly about their psychedelic industry experience and which specific carriers they work with before engaging them.

When public information may be enough

Oregon’s workers’ compensation requirements are administered through the Department of Consumer and Business Services at dcbs.oregon.gov. Colorado’s workers’ compensation system is at cdle.colorado.gov. ORS 475A.395 authorizes OPS to require insurance from licensees. Specialty carrier and broker information is available directly from the carriers and brokers listed above.

When you should speak with a lawyer

An operator who has received a coverage denial from an insurer and believes the denial is wrongful should consult a lawyer with insurance coverage litigation experience before accepting the denial. An operator who is reviewing insurance requirements in a lease or facilitator contract should have those requirements reviewed by an attorney to confirm they are being met by the operator’s actual coverage. An operator who has experienced a client incident and is uncertain which coverage applies should consult an attorney before filing a claim or making any statements to an insurer.
