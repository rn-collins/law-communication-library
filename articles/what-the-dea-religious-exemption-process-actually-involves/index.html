What the DEA religious exemption process actually involves

Last reviewed: August 2026 · Psychedelic law changes quickly — verify current status before relying on this page.

Who this is for: Groups and leaders considering a federal religious exemption to use a psychedelic sacrament, and anyone trying to understand whether the DEA process is a realistic path.

The short answer

The DEA has a process for petitioning the federal government for a religious exemption from the Controlled Substances Act, adopted after the Supreme Court’s O Centro decision. A group or person submits a written petition describing the religion, the sacrament, and the controls around its use, and the DEA evaluates it under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act while raising its own concerns about diversion, health, and international treaty obligations. The process is demanding, slow, and rarely successful, and filing a petition does not authorize use of the substance while the petition is pending. Most groups that want lawful religious use either never complete this process or end up in court.

Where the process comes from

After the Supreme Court held in O Centro that the Religious Freedom Restoration Act can protect a church’s sacramental use of a Schedule I substance, the DEA issued guidance in 2009 describing how a group can petition the agency for a religious exemption. The guidance is the agency’s attempt to put the court’s standard into an administrative process. How a petition works

A petition is a written request submitted to the DEA. There is no form to fill out; the petitioner writes to the agency and makes the case for an exemption. The petition goes to the DEA’s Diversion Control Division, which reviews it and can ask for more information. The agency can grant the exemption, often with conditions, deny it, or leave it pending. A petitioner who disagrees with a denial, or who waits without a response, can take the matter to federal court.

What a petition has to address

The petition has to give the agency enough to apply the Religious Freedom Restoration Act’s test. That means describing the religious belief and showing it is sincere, identifying the sacrament and explaining its place in the practice, and describing who uses it and how. The agency also looks for the controls the group has in place: how it screens participants, how it supervises ceremonies, how it sources and secures the substance, and how it keeps the sacrament from reaching people outside the religious practice. A petition that cannot show a sincere religion and real safeguards has little chance.

What the agency weighs against the petition

Even a strong petition meets the government’s asserted interests. The DEA raises the risk that the substance will be diverted to non-religious use, the health and safety of participants, and the country’s obligations under international drug treaties. Psilocybin, DMT, and mescaline are controlled under the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances, and the agency treats compliance with that treaty as a serious interest. Peyote used by the Native American Church occupies a settled position that other substances do not, so a petition for ayahuasca, psilocybin, or another sacrament starts without that history.

Petitioning does not authorize use in the meantime

Filing a petition does not make the use legal while the agency considers it. A group that submits a petition and continues to hold ceremonies is using a Schedule I substance without authorization throughout the process, which can take a long time. The petition is a request for permission, not a grant of it, and the federal prohibition continues to apply until an exemption is actually issued.

How rarely it works, and the role of litigation

Exemptions through this process are rare. The clearest protections for non-peyote sacraments have come through litigation rather than the administrative process: O Centro’s accommodation for ayahuasca came from a Supreme Court case, and some Santo Daime communities obtained protection through court action. Where groups have relied on the DEA process, results have been discouraging. Courts have reviewed DEA denials, and in the Soul Quest litigation an appeals court upheld the agency’s denial of an ayahuasca church’s petition. What an exemption looks like if granted

An exemption, when granted, comes with conditions rather than a free hand. The group may have to register with the DEA, secure the substance, keep records, limit the source and quantity, restrict use to the religious ceremony, and accept oversight. The accommodation O Centro received operates under such conditions. An exemption authorizes a specific group’s specific practice; it does not make the substance legal in general or transfer to another group.

When public information may be enough

The DEA’s 2009 guidance and the leading court decisions are public, and a person can read them to understand what the process requires and how seldom it succeeds. Anyone weighing whether to pursue an exemption can learn the shape of the process from public sources before committing to it.

When you should speak with a lawyer

Preparing a religious exemption petition, or deciding whether to pursue one at all, calls for a lawyer who knows this area. The petition has to satisfy a demanding legal test and anticipate the government’s interests, and the choice between petitioning, litigating, or doing neither carries real consequences for everyone involved in the group. A lawyer can assess whether a sincere and well-controlled practice has a realistic chance and what the group risks while it waits.
